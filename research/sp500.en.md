**The S&P 500 index** — tracking 500 of America's largest companies — represents approximately 80–85% of the entire U.S. stock market and about 40–45% of the global stock market capitalization. The index has delivered ~10% annualized returns since the beginning of the century, and became the default recommendation for retail investors.

Most investors think <code>SPY</code> and <code>VOO</code> are "basically the same," but:

- Over 30 years, that assumption costs **$30,196** on a $100k investment.
- Buying the wrong domicile in Europe can cost another **€7,500+** per €100k in avoidable tax drag.
- ETF or mutual fund? Physical or synthetic? Accumulating or distributing?
- Why not just buy it on Revolut or Kraken? What's the difference?
- EU investors can't buy <code>VOO</code> — or can they?
- What about leveraged ETFs? What about options?
- Over 250 funds have '500' in the name, so where to even start?

The good news is: you only have to make this decision once.

## How the benchmark was born

**1957**: Standard & Poor's introduces the S&P 500 Index. For nearly two decades, it exists purely as a benchmark. The constituent companies represent approximately 90% of U.S. market capitalization.

**1976**: Vanguard launches the First Index Investment Trust (now <code>VFIAX</code>) — the first mutual fund allowing ordinary investors to buy the entire S&P 500 with a single purchase.

**1993**: State Street launches the SPDR S&P 500 ETF (<code>SPY</code>), creating the first exchange-traded fund and transforming the S&P 500 from a performance benchmark into an instantly tradable security.

**2000s**: "Invest in an S&P 500 index fund" becomes default advice from financial advisors, personal finance authors, and Warren Buffett.

**2025**: Companies in the index account for 80% of the U.S. and 50% of the global stock market, making it the most prominent benchmark for the modern economy. The average annual return of ~10% secures its status as the default recommendation for retail investors.

## Why S&P 500?

The S&P 500 is not the only game in town:

**Dow Jones Industrial Average** — 30 stocks, price-weighted, US. The oldest (1896) and most-quoted index, but arguably the least useful. Its archaic price-weighting methodology means a $400 stock has 10x the influence of a $40 stock regardless of company size. This was an easy-to-maintain proxy before computers; today it simply doesn't represent the market correctly.

**Nasdaq-100** — 100 stocks, market-cap weighted, US. Tracks the 100 largest non-financial companies on the Nasdaq exchange. In practice, this gives concentrated exposure to technology (around 50% of the index), which especially in recent years many investors prefer. However, it's not fully representative — both size- and sector-wise — if broad U.S. exposure is what you're looking for.

**Russell 1000** — 1,000 stocks, market-cap weighted, US. Tracks the largest 1,000 U.S. companies, representing approximately 92% of the U.S. market. It overlaps heavily with the S&P 500 (which holds about 500 of the same companies) but includes an additional 500 mid-cap stocks. The practical difference in returns is minimal — typically within 0.2–0.3% annually.

**Russell 2000** — 2,000 stocks, market-cap weighted, US. Tracks small-cap U.S. companies — specifically, companies ranked 1,001 through 3,000 by market cap. These represent only about 8% of total U.S. market capitalization but offer diversification away from mega-caps. It's a complement to S&P 500 exposure, not a replacement.

**MSCI World** — ~1,500 stocks, market-cap weighted, 23 developed countries. Provides exposure to large and mid-cap stocks across 23 developed markets: U.S., Europe, Japan, Canada, Australia, and others — but excludes emerging markets (China, India, Brazil) entirely. MSCI World often feels redundant (~70% of what you already have in the S&P 500).

**MSCI ACWI (All Country World)** — ~2,900 stocks, market-cap weighted, 47 countries. Combines developed markets and emerging markets (China, India, Taiwan, South Korea, Brazil) into a single index covering approximately 85% of global market capitalization. This is the closest thing to "buy the entire global stock market" in a single holding. U.S. stocks still dominate at around 60–65% of the index and often drive its growth.

Over time, as a brand, the S&P 500 won. Its combination of diversification, liquidity, and low cost became a default recommendation for both retail and institutional investors.

## What you're buying

The S&P 500 isn't simply "the 500 largest U.S. companies." A committee at S&P Global selects them based on market capitalization (minimum ~$18 billion), profitability (four consecutive profitable quarters), adequate liquidity, and U.S. domicile. Some question its neutrality and criteria, but over its 69-year history it has become the trusted brand name. That's why to this day, despite the methodology being easier to replicate than ever, many funds still pay for a Standard & Poor's license.

> Market-cap weighting means the bigger the company, the more funds are allocated to it in the basket. As of late 2024, the top 10 holdings (Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon, Meta, Alphabet, Berkshire Hathaway, Broadcom, Tesla, JPMorgan) represent approximately 35% of the entire index.

The committee adjusts the list quarterly (March, June, September, December), adding and removing companies as their characteristics change. All tracking funds incur small costs when adjusting their portfolios to match these changes — this is one source of tracking difference even between similarly weighted funds.

## United States

If you have a US brokerage account, you have access to the cheapest and most liquid S&P 500 products in the world. There are two questions to answer:

- ETF or mutual fund?
- Tax-advantaged or taxable account?

Let's start with the products, then I'll show you how to choose between them.

### ETFs

Four ETFs dominate S&P 500 tracking:

| Ticker | Issuer | Exp. Ratio | AUM | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **<code>VOO</code>** | Vanguard | 0.03% | ~$1.6T | Largest ETF in the world |
| **<code>IVV</code>** | BlackRock (iShares) | 0.03% | ~$680B | Daily holdings transparency |
| **<code>SPLG</code>** | State Street (SPDR) | 0.02% | ~$75B | Cheapest US-listed, low share price |
| **<code>SPY</code>** | State Street (SPDR) | 0.0945% | ~$680B | The liquidity king |

<code>VOO</code>, <code>IVV</code>, and <code>SPLG</code> use modern open-end ETF structures that allow dividend reinvestment within the fund and securities lending income. <code>SPLG</code> edges out on cost (0.02%) and has a lower share price, which matters if you're buying whole shares without fractional share access.

<code>SPY</code> is the outlier. Its 1993-era unit investment trust structure doesn't allow dividend reinvestment within the fund — yet it still charges 4.7x more than <code>SPLG</code>. So why does it exist?

Liquidity. <code>SPY</code> trades $50–60 billion daily, making it the most liquid equity security on earth. It has the deepest options market, the tightest bid-ask spreads, and can absorb massive institutional orders without moving the price. If you're running complex options strategies or moving millions of dollars, <code>SPY</code>'s liquidity premium is worth paying.

For everyone else, it's not. On every $100,000 of your investment earning 10% annually over 30 years, the difference between <code>SPY</code>'s 0.0945% fee and <code>VOO</code>'s 0.03% equals approximately $30,000 in lost wealth.

#### The verdict: <code>VOO</code>

<code>SPLG</code> charges 0.02% versus <code>VOO</code>'s 0.03% — so why not pick the cheaper option?

Because expense ratio isn't everything. What matters is tracking difference: how closely the fund's actual returns match the index. Factors like securities lending income, dividend timing, and rebalancing efficiency can easily offset a 0.01% fee gap.

In practice, <code>VOO</code> marginally outperforms other options in this set, wins on liquidity, and carries Vanguard's reputation for shareholder-friendly practices.

Quick summary:

- **<code>VOO</code>** → Default choice for buy-and-hold investors
- **<code>IVV</code>** → Only if you're in the iShares ecosystem
- **<code>SPLG</code>** → Only if your broker doesn't support fractional shares
- **<code>SPY</code>** → Only if you trade options

### When mutual funds make sense

There are three reasons to pick mutual funds over ETFs:

**<code>FXAIX</code> is the cheapest S&P 500 product available anywhere** — 0.015%, half the cost of <code>VOO</code>. The catch: you can only hold it at Fidelity. If you're a Fidelity customer with no plans to leave, <code>FXAIX</code> is mathematically the best option.

**401(k) plans.** Most plan menus are built around mutual funds. And since 401(k)s are tax-advantaged, you lose nothing — the ETF tax-efficiency edge disappears inside a tax-sheltered account anyway.

**Automatic dollar-amount investing.** Want to invest exactly $500 on the 1st of every month? Mutual funds handle fractional shares natively. Most brokers now offer fractional ETF shares too, but the experience is smoother with mutual funds.

There are also disadvantages:

**Tax inefficiency in taxable accounts.** When mutual fund investors redeem shares, the fund may need to sell holdings to raise cash — triggering capital gains for all shareholders. ETFs avoid this through their in-kind redemption mechanism.

**Minimum investment requirements.** <code>FXAIX</code> and <code>SWPPX</code> have no minimum, but Vanguard's <code>VFIAX</code> requires $3,000 to start. If you're just beginning with smaller amounts, ETFs let you start with whatever you have.

**Trading limitations.** Mutual funds trade once daily at the closing price, and you can't use limit orders. For long-term investors buying and holding, this is a feature — it removes the temptation to time the market or panic-sell mid-day.

#### Zero-fee alternatives

The S&P 500 is a trademarked index. Fund providers pay licensing fees to use the name. A few alternatives dodge this cost by tracking nearly identical indices:

- **<code>FNILX</code>** (Fidelity mutual fund) — 0.00% TER. Tracks the Fidelity US Large Cap Index.
- **<code>BKLC</code>** (BNY Mellon ETF) — 0.00% TER. Tracks the Solactive GBS US 500.

<code>FNILX</code> charges literally nothing. It tracks a Fidelity-created index that mirrors the S&P 500 with one difference: no profitability requirement. This means <code>FNILX</code> might include large unprofitable companies before they officially enter the S&P 500 (as happened with Tesla before its 2020 inclusion). In practice, annual returns differ from the S&P 500 by less than 0.2%.

Limitation: <code>FNILX</code> can only be held at Fidelity and cannot transfer to other brokers.

<code>BKLC</code> offers zero fees in ETF form — meaning intraday trading, portability between brokerages, and ETF tax efficiency. It tracks the 500 largest US companies mechanically, without committee discretion.

Are these worth it? The savings over <code>VOO</code> (0.03%) amount to $30 per year per $100,000 invested. Meaningful over decades, but not life-changing. If you're already at Fidelity, <code>FNILX</code> is a no-brainer. Otherwise, the practical difference between <code>BKLC</code> and <code>VOO</code> is negligible.

#### Tax considerations for US investors

:::picker{us_account}

:::when{us_account=taxable}

**In taxable accounts, ETFs win.** ETFs have a structural advantage: the in-kind creation/redemption mechanism. When investors sell ETF shares, they sell to other investors on the exchange — no fund-level transaction occurs. This means no taxable events for remaining shareholders. Over the past five years, fewer than 5% of equity ETFs distributed capital gains, versus 57% of equity mutual funds.

**Watch out for wash sales.** Planning to tax-loss harvest by selling <code>VOO</code> at a loss and buying <code>IVV</code>? The IRS may disallow your loss. These funds track identical indices, making them "substantially identical" under wash sale rules. Safer swaps: a Russell 1000 ETF (<code>IWB</code>) or total market fund (<code>VTI</code>) — similar exposure, different index.

:::

:::when{us_account=tax_advantaged}

**In tax-advantaged accounts (IRA, Roth IRA, HSA), it doesn't matter.** Inside a tax-sheltered wrapper, there's no tax difference between ETFs and mutual funds. Choose based on cost and convenience alone.

If you're at Fidelity, **<code>FXAIX</code> (0.015%) or <code>FNILX</code> (0.00%)** are mathematically cheaper than <code>VOO</code> — both are mutual funds, Fidelity-only, and can't be transferred to other brokers.

For ETF portability: <code>VOO</code> or <code>SPLG</code>. Either works fine in tax-advantaged accounts.

:::

:::when{us_account=employer}

**In a 401(k), your options are whatever the plan menu offers.** Pick the lowest-cost S&P 500 fund available — usually Vanguard's <code>VIIIX</code>, Fidelity's <code>FXAIX</code>, or a similar institutional share class. Since 401(k)s are tax-sheltered, the ETF vs. mutual fund distinction is irrelevant. Fees are all that matter.

Once you leave that employer, roll the 401(k) into an IRA where you have broader selection.

:::

#### Quick decision guide for US investors

- **You're at Fidelity** → <code>FXAIX</code> (0.015%) or <code>FNILX</code> (0.00%)
- **Taxable account, long-term** → <code>SPLG</code> (0.02%) or <code>VOO</code> (0.03%)
- **401(k)** → Lowest-cost S&P 500 fund available in your plan
- **Active trader / options** → <code>SPY</code> (liquidity justifies the cost)
- **Maximum cost minimization** → <code>FNILX</code> (Fidelity) or <code>BKLC</code> (any broker)

That covers the US side. For everyone else, the choice is harder — and the stakes are higher.

## Europe & UK

If you're European and you try to buy <code>VOO</code> through your regular EU broker, you'll hit a wall. The reason is absurd enough to be worth understanding.

Since 2018, PRIIPs regulation has required every fund sold to European retail investors to publish a Key Information Document (KID) that projects future performance. US securities law treats such projections as litigation risk — US issuers refuse to publish them. So any EU-regulated broker must block the trade.

The irony: the same regulation that blocks you from buying <code>VOO</code> for 0.03% a year happily lets you buy a leveraged CFD on the S&P 500 from a broker whose own disclaimer says 74% of retail clients lose money.

There are three real paths around this:

1. **Path A: Use a UCITS ETF.** The European mirror universe. ~99% of European investors take this route.
2. **Path B: Use an EU-regulated broker in Cyprus or Malta** (Freedom24, Exante) that offers US ETFs to retail anyway.
3. **Path C: Open an account with a US-regulated broker** (IBKR LLC, Schwab International, tastytrade, Firstrade, TradeStation, Alpaca) — PRIIPs doesn't apply.

### Path A: UCITS ETFs

The UCITS universe is in some ways better than the US one. Not cheaper — but more tax-efficient for non-US investors, almost always accumulating (no manual dividend reinvestment), and with structural features US investors literally cannot access.

The downside: there are more dials to turn, and turning them wrong costs real money.

### The four dials

Every European S&P 500 ETF sits at some combination of four choices:

1. **Domicile** — Ireland or Luxembourg (almost always)
2. **Replication** — Physical or synthetic (swap-based)
3. **Distribution** — Accumulating or distributing
4. **Currency** — USD-denominated, EUR-denominated, or EUR-hedged

Get domicile wrong and you lose ~15 basis points a year to extra US withholding tax. Get distribution wrong and you may pay tax you didn't need to, in the wrong country, at the wrong time. The other two are more about preference than cost.

Let's go through them.

### Dial 1: Domicile — the Ireland advantage

The US charges a 30% withholding tax on dividends paid to non-US investors. Ireland has a bilateral tax treaty with the US that reduces this to 15% for funds structured as corporations. Luxembourg does not.

In practice this means:

- **Ireland-domiciled S&P 500 ETF** → 15% tax drag on dividends
- **Luxembourg-domiciled S&P 500 ETF** → 30% tax drag on dividends
- **US-domiciled ETF (if you somehow own one)** → 30% tax drag, plus potential US estate tax on holdings above $60,000

With the S&P 500 currently yielding around 1.3%, the Ireland/Luxembourg difference is ~20 basis points per year. Compounded over 30 years on €100k, that's roughly €100k in foregone returns — bigger than most fee differences combined.

About 70% of European ETF assets are domiciled in Ireland precisely because of this treaty. You'll recognize Irish-domiciled ETFs by their ISIN starting with `IE` (Luxembourg is `LU`, Germany `DE`, France `FR`).

**Verdict: Always prefer Ireland-domiciled for S&P 500 exposure.** This is the single most important dial.

One wrinkle: synthetic (swap-based) ETFs can actually sidestep withholding tax entirely thanks to a specific IRS carve-out (Section 871(m)), capturing essentially the full gross dividend. More on that in Dial 2.

### Dial 2: Replication — physical or synthetic

**Physical replication** means the fund actually owns the 500 underlying stocks in the right proportions. If you want Apple exposure, the fund holds Apple shares. Simple, transparent, easy to understand.

**Synthetic replication** means the fund holds a basket of collateral (often unrelated equities) and enters into a swap contract with an investment bank. The bank pays the fund the exact return of the S&P 500 in exchange for the collateral's return, plus a small fee.

For most asset classes, physical is the default because synthetic introduces counterparty risk — the risk that the swap counterparty fails. But for the S&P 500, synthetic has a structural advantage:

A physical Irish-domiciled ETF pays 15% US withholding tax on its dividends and captures ~85% of the gross yield. A synthetic ETF, thanks to a carve-out in US tax law for swap-based access to indices with US equity underlyings, can capture essentially 100%. On a 1.3% dividend yield, that's another ~20 basis points of annual outperformance.

BlackRock — the world's most staunch advocate of physical replication — launched a synthetic S&P 500 ETF (<code>I500</code>) in 2020. That alone tells you everything about how strong the structural advantage is.

Counterparty risk is real but actively managed. Invesco's synthetic S&P 500 ETF (<code>SPXS</code>) uses six different investment banks as swap counterparties, resets exposures on tight triggers, and publishes holdings daily. EU regulations cap swap exposure at 10% of fund assets. In practice, after 15+ years of operation, no major synthetic S&P 500 ETF has lost investor money to counterparty failure.

**Verdict:**

- **Physical** if you value transparency and sleep-at-night simplicity
- **Synthetic** if you want every last basis point of performance and are comfortable with the modern swap structure

### Dial 3: Accumulating or distributing

This one depends entirely on your tax jurisdiction.

**Accumulating ETFs** (suffix "Acc" in the name) reinvest dividends automatically inside the fund. You never see cash. Your ETF shares simply grow slightly more than the index price would suggest.

**Distributing ETFs** (suffix "Dist") pay dividends to your brokerage account quarterly, which you can reinvest manually or spend.

On paper, accumulating is almost always better for long-term investors:

- No friction — dividends compound immediately, without needing cash to accumulate to the price of a full share
- No tax leakage — in some countries, distributed dividends are taxed immediately, while accumulated dividends are only taxed on sale
- Simpler paperwork — no dividend transactions to report in countries that require per-transaction reporting

But this varies dramatically by country. See the jurisdiction notes below.

**Default verdict: Accumulating** — unless your country specifically penalizes it (Germany does, via the Vorabpauschale — but even then, accumulating often still wins long-term).

### Dial 4: Currency

The S&P 500 is a USD index. Every European-listed S&P 500 ETF holds (directly or via swap) USD-denominated assets. The currency you buy and sell the ETF in only affects the transaction, not the underlying exposure.

In other words: buying <code>VUAA</code> in EUR on Xetra or in USD on the London Stock Exchange gives you the exact same underlying exposure. Price movements in EUR/USD will affect your returns either way.

**Currency hedging** (EUR-hedged share classes, suffixed "EUR Hdg") uses forward contracts to neutralize EUR/USD movements. This is useful if you're planning to spend your money in EUR within a short time horizon. For a 30-year buy-and-hold, hedging typically costs more than the volatility reduction is worth:

- SPDR S&P 500 EUR Hedged (<code>SPPE</code>) — 0.05% TER vs 0.03% unhedged
- iShares S&P 500 EUR Hedged (<code>IUSE</code>) — 0.20% TER vs 0.07% unhedged

**Verdict: Don't hedge unless you're close to spending the money in EUR.** Over long horizons, currency volatility tends to wash out and hedging costs compound against you.

**Default pick: <code>SPYL</code>.** Cheapest, simplest, and matches what most people mean when they say "I want an S&P 500 ETF."

### Paths B and C: accessing US-domiciled ETFs

I've covered the full case for Path B and Path C — including the sovereignty argument — in a separate essay: [How to Buy US ETFs as a European](https://sovereignoptimist.substack.com/p/how-to-buy-us-etfs-as-a-european). Two points worth surfacing here, because they're the only principles you actually need to decide:

**Wrapper first, then US broker.** Fill your country's tax wrapper (ISA, PEA, IKE, investeerimiskonto, and so on) before opening a US account. The wrapper gives tax efficiency the US account can't; the US account gives jurisdictional diversification the wrapper can't. Both compound.

**Check your US estate tax treaty before Path C.** The US levies estate tax on US-listed assets held by non-US persons above a $60,000 exemption. Treaties raise this meaningfully for the UK, Germany, France, Ireland, Austria, Denmark, Finland, Greece, Italy, Netherlands, and Switzerland. For Poland, Estonia, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, Czech Republic, Slovakia, Bulgaria, and most of Eastern Europe, the default $60k limit stands — and the rate above it can reach 40%. A $70k <code>VOO</code> position at a US broker in Warsaw or Tallinn is a serious tax landmine for your heirs. A <code>CSPX</code> position through a Warsaw-based broker is not.

### Country-specific notes

Your fund choice is the easy part. Where it gets hard is your *account* — the wrapper you hold the fund in. Most of Europe has some version of a tax-advantaged investment account, and using it properly is usually worth more than any fee optimization.

:::picker{country}

:::when{country=at}

**Austria** — 27.5% KESt on dividends and realized capital gains. Accumulating ETFs are fine as long as they have "reporting fund" status (<code>CSPX</code>, <code>SPYL</code>, and <code>VUAA</code> all qualify). Austrian brokers handle tax automatically; foreign brokers require you to self-report via E1kv. US estate tax treaty exists.

:::

:::when{country=bg}

**Bulgaria** — 10% flat capital gains tax, one of the lowest in the EU. No special wrapper needed; the headline rate is already attractive. No US estate tax treaty — Path C holders should stay below $60k.

:::

:::when{country=cz}

**Czech Republic** — 0% capital gains tax on securities held longer than 3 years (the "time test"). Makes accumulating UCITS ETFs extremely efficient for long-term holders. No US estate tax treaty.

:::

:::when{country=dk}

**Denmark** — The most punitive ETF regime in Europe. ETFs are taxed under *Lagerprincippet* (mark-to-market annually, whether you sold or not) at 27%/42% progressive rates. The Aktiesparekonto (ASK) wrapper caps tax at 17% up to ~166,200 DKK (2025 limit). US estate tax treaty exists — but Danish tax is usually the bigger problem.

:::

:::when{country=ee}

**Estonia** — The investeerimiskonto (investment account) lets you defer income tax indefinitely on reinvested profits. You're only taxed when withdrawals exceed contributions. Functionally, a regular brokerage account with ISA-like deferral and no annual cap. Lightyear and LHV handle reporting to EMTA automatically. No US estate tax treaty — stay on the UCITS side for Path C alternatives.

:::

:::when{country=fr}

**France** — The PEA (Plan d'Épargne en Actions) exempts gains from income tax after 5 years; 17.2% social charges still apply at withdrawal. The PEA only accepts funds with ≥75% European equity exposure, so S&P 500 goes in via "PEA-eligible" synthetic ETFs like Amundi <code>PE500</code> or BNP Paribas <code>ESE</code>. TER is slightly higher (~0.15%) but the PEA tax advantage more than compensates. US estate tax treaty exists.

:::

:::when{country=de}

**Germany** — Accumulating ETFs trigger Vorabpauschale (an annual "deemed dividend" tax), calculated from the Basiszins — 3.2% for 2026, making the January deduction meaningful. Equity ETFs qualify for 30% Teilfreistellung on both Vorabpauschale and capital gains. Net effect: accumulating still beats distributing long-term, but keep cash in your broker account every January for the automatic collection. US estate tax treaty exists and raises exemption to ~$13.6M.

:::

:::when{country=gr}

**Greece** — 0% capital gains tax on UCITS ETFs for private investors. Dividends are taxed at 5%. Few strong local brokers — IBKR and XTB dominate for S&P 500 exposure. US estate tax treaty exists.

:::

:::when{country=ie}

**Ireland** — A uniquely painful regime. UCITS ETFs are subject to 41% exit tax (dropping to 38% in January 2026) *and* a "deemed disposal" every 8 years that taxes unrealized gains. Losses on ETFs cannot be offset against gains elsewhere. The irony: Irish tax residents get worse treatment on Irish-domiciled ETFs than the rest of the world does. Pension wrappers (PRSA, ARF) are exempt. Given the domestic regime, Path C actually deserves serious consideration for Irish residents — and the US estate tax treaty raises the exemption to the full amount.

:::

:::when{country=it}

**Italy** — 26% flat tax on realized gains and distributions. Accumulating ETFs defer this until sale. IVAFE (0.2% annual wealth tax) applies to all foreign-held securities, reported via the RW form — this applies whether you hold at an EU or US broker. US estate tax treaty exists.

:::

:::when{country=nl}

**Netherlands** — Box 3 taxes assumed returns on wealth rather than realized gains, so the accumulating/distributing distinction matters less than elsewhere. The regime is transitioning to real-returns taxation by 2027, which will change the optimal strategy. US estate tax treaty exists.

:::

:::when{country=pl}

**Poland** — Three wrappers stack beautifully: IKE (capital gains exempt at retirement), IKZE (up-front deduction on contributions), and the new OKI launching July 2026 (up to 100,000 zł/year fully exempt). Using all three effectively eliminates the 19% Belka tax for most middle-class investors. Favor accumulating UCITS (<code>CSPX</code>, <code>SPYL</code>) via DM BOŚ or mBank. No US estate tax treaty — keep any Path C exposure under $60k.

:::

:::when{country=pt}

**Portugal** — 28% flat tax on capital gains and dividends. Short-term gains (<365 days) are aggregated into progressive income tax for higher earners. The NHR regime closed to new entrants in 2023; its successor IFICI exempts most foreign-source investment income for qualifying researchers and skilled professionals. US estate tax treaty does *not* exist — Portugal is a Path C trap for anything meaningful.

:::

:::when{country=sk}

**Slovakia** — 0% capital gains tax on securities held longer than 1 year. Under 1 year, taxed at 19%. Extremely efficient for long-term holders. No US estate tax treaty.

:::

:::when{country=es}

**Spain** — Capital gains taxed at 19–28% progressive. Accumulating defers the tax until sale. "Traspaso" allows tax-free switching between Spanish-registered mutual funds — but not between foreign UCITS ETFs. Trade-off: traspaso flexibility comes at the cost of higher Spanish mutual fund TERs. No US estate tax treaty — Path C is a trap above $60k.

:::

:::when{country=se}

**Sweden** — ISK (Investeringssparkonto) applies a flat ~1% annual tax on average account value in place of capital gains tax. Extraordinarily tax-efficient. Available via Avanza and Nordnet. Without ISK, the standard 30% CGT applies. No US estate tax treaty.

:::

:::when{country=ch}

**Switzerland** — 0% capital gains tax for private investors (provided you don't cross the "professional trader" line: trade frequency, holding period, borrowing, speculative instruments). Dividends taxed at marginal income rates. Wealth tax applies at cantonal rates (0.1–1% typically). Pillar 3a is the main wrapper — CHF 7,258/year in 2026, fully deductible, tax-deferred growth. US estate tax treaty exists. One of the best private-investor jurisdictions globally.

:::

:::when{country=uk}

**United Kingdom** — ISA (£20,000/year, fully tax-free inside) and SIPP (pension, tax relief on contributions). Any UCITS ETF with UK Reporting Fund status works — <code>VUAG</code>, <code>CSPX</code>, <code>SPYL</code> all qualify. Use your ISA allowance before touching a taxable General Investment Account. US estate tax treaty treats UK residents as if they had the full $13.6M exemption; Path C is estate-tax-safe, though it forfeits ISA/SIPP benefits.

:::

The general principle across Europe: **wrapper first, fund second.** A <code>CSPX</code> in a tax-advantaged account almost always beats <code>SPYL</code> in a taxable account.

:::

:::when{region=ca}

## Canada

Three CAD-denominated S&P 500 ETFs at ~0.09% MER. Account type matters more than ticker choice.

**Canadian-listed options (all ~0.09% MER):** <code>VFV</code> (Vanguard), <code>XUS</code> (iShares), <code>ZSP</code> (BMO). Virtually identical — pick one and move on.

**In an RRSP:** Hold the US-listed <code>VOO</code> directly. The Canada-US tax treaty exempts RRSPs from the 15% US dividend withholding tax — a recurring saving on top of <code>VOO</code>'s lower MER (0.03% vs. 0.09%). This is the one scenario where going US-listed makes clear sense for Canadians.

**In a TFSA or RESP:** The RRSP withholding exemption does *not* apply. Stick with Canadian-listed <code>VFV</code> / <code>XUS</code> / <code>ZSP</code>. The 15% US WHT is embedded either way, and you avoid T1135 foreign-asset reporting (triggered at $100k+ cost basis).

**In a taxable account:** Canadian-listed, recover the 15% WHT via the foreign tax credit on your return. Or use a swap-based structure like <code>HXS</code> (Horizons) that eliminates dividend taxation entirely — but carries counterparty risk.

**US estate tax:** The Canada-US treaty provides Unified Credit relief, but US-listed assets above thresholds can still create estate exposure. Canadian-listed ETFs are exempt from US estate tax entirely.

:::

:::when{region=au}

## Australia / New Zealand

Two distinct options: locally-domiciled for simplicity, or cross-listed for fee savings with paperwork.

**Australian-domiciled:** <code>IVV.AX</code> (iShares, 0.04% MER) or <code>SPY.AX</code> (SPDR). No W-8BEN required, simpler estate planning, dividend reinvestment plans available. Default choice for most Australian investors.

**Cross-listed from the US:** <code>VTS.AX</code> (Vanguard Total US Market, 0.03% MER). Cheaper by 1 bp, but requires W-8BEN-E every 3 years via Computershare. Also US-domiciled, raising US estate tax considerations.

**Super (superannuation):** Your main tax wrapper. 15% tax on earnings during accumulation, 0% in pension phase. Holdings in super are owned by the trust, not you personally — US-domiciled funds inside super are exempt from US estate tax entirely. A meaningful planning advantage.

**Outside super:** Marginal income tax rates apply, with a 50% CGT discount on holdings over 12 months.

Australia has a US estate tax treaty, but it's less generous than the UK/Germany versions. Consider this before accumulating significant US-domiciled holdings personally (outside super).

:::

:::when{region=other}

## Everywhere else

The default is almost always Ireland-domiciled UCITS via IBKR, with a few country-specific refinements.

**Singapore and Hong Kong:** 0% CGT locally. Ireland-domiciled UCITS (<code>CSPX</code>, <code>SPYL</code>) via IBKR, Tiger, or Saxo is the consensus default. US-listed ETFs trigger 30% US WHT (no treaty) and US estate tax above $60k — both severe enough to favor UCITS.

**UAE / Saudi / Qatar:** 0% CGT, no income tax. UCITS via IBKR dominates. Same US estate tax trap — stay in Irish domicile.

**Japan:** 20.315% CGT, but the NISA wrapper provides tax-free growth up to annual limits. Local S&P 500 mutual funds via Monex or Rakuten are popular. eMAXIS Slim S&P 500 is the low-cost local default.

**Latin America:** US broker access via IBKR or local brokers feeding into US markets is common. UCITS often impractical — most LatAm brokers offer US-listed ETFs directly. Withholding tax and US estate tax apply; check your country's treaty status.

**Digital nomads / tax residents of "nowhere":** The trickiest case. UCITS via IBKR is the safest default because it avoids the worst US estate tax exposure while preserving portability. But tax residency determines your actual obligation — consult a cross-border specialist before assuming anything.

:::

## The detours

People ask me about these constantly. Short answer: most of them are worse than a boring UCITS ETF. Long answer below.

:::optional{Revolut}

### Revolut (and the neobank investing question)

Revolut deserves its own section because it's neither a detour nor a traditional broker.

Revolut is a licensed bank/e-money institution that added investing on top of its payments business. The actual brokerage is outsourced: DriveWealth (US) handles trades for US customers; Revolut Securities Europe UAB handles EU customers via similar partner arrangements. You're a client of Revolut, who is a client of a broker-dealer.

For fractional shares specifically — which is most of what people buy on Revolut given the UX — you're the "beneficial owner of a part of the actual share instrument in proportion to your fractional interest." In practical terms, that means you have a contractual claim against Revolut/DriveWealth rather than direct beneficial ownership via a traditional nominee structure. Whole shares are cleaner, but the app doesn't meaningfully distinguish between the two.

Three consequences flow from this architecture:

- **Transfer-out is limited.** You can ACATS whole shares to another broker, but fractional shares get liquidated. If you accumulate €50k of <code>VUAA</code> half a share at a time, you can't move those positions cleanly to IBKR later.
- **No tax-wrapper support.** Revolut can't open an ISA for you in most plan tiers (they do in the UK now, but limited), can't run a PEA in France, can't be used as investeerimiskonto in Estonia. You're stuck with a plain taxable account.
- **Mixed product interface.** Stocks, crypto, CFDs (in some regions), commodities, and cash all live in the same app, nudging users toward behavior that long-term S&P 500 investing specifically doesn't reward.

None of this is fraud or even a red flag. Revolut is a perfectly legitimate way to buy your first €500 of an S&P 500 ETF at age 22. It's just not the right structure for serious, long-term, tax-optimized wealth accumulation.

**Verdict: Fine for starter portfolios. Outgrow it by the time the balance is meaningful.** Transfer to an investment-first broker (IBKR, DEGIRO, Saxo, Lightyear, Trading 212, or your country's equivalent with proper wrapper support) before you hit the point where transfer friction becomes a real cost.

:::

:::optional{Trading 212 / eToro}

### Trading 212, eToro, and other app brokers

These are real brokers in the traditional sense — licensed investment firms (FCA, CySEC, or equivalent), using nominee custody, with proper beneficial ownership. Trading 212 offers UK ISAs. eToro, despite its CFD-heavy marketing, operates a real equities business alongside.

Fee-wise, they're competitive or free for UCITS ETF trades. The main gotchas are:

- **FX costs** — Trading 212's 0.15% FX fee on currency conversion adds up on DCA
- **Wrapper availability** — patchy by country, worth checking before committing
- **Mixed product temptation** — like Revolut, these apps keep "trading" and "investing" in the same interface, which is not what you want psychologically for 30-year buy-and-hold

Interactive Brokers and DEGIRO charge lower FX spreads and tighter commissions at the cost of less forgiving UX. For six-figure portfolios and above, the difference becomes meaningful.

**Verdict: Broker choice matters less than people think, provided you avoid the obvious traps** (high per-trade fees, no wrapper support, no ACATS-out). Pick the broker with the best wrapper support for your country and move on.

:::

:::optional{Kraken xStocks}

### xStocks (<code>VOOx</code>)

Kraken (and a few other crypto exchanges) offer "tokenized stocks" — blockchain tokens that track real stock prices 1:1. <code>VOOx</code> tracks the price of <code>VOO</code>. You can buy it 24/5 (24/7 on-chain), fractionally from $1, using either USD, EUR, or crypto.

Sounds great. Here's what you actually get:

- Issued by Backed Assets (JE) Limited in Jersey
- Backed 1:1 by real <code>VOO</code> shares held by a regulated third-party custodian
- Transferred as SPL tokens on Solana or ERC-20 on Ethereum
- Offered via Payward Digital Solutions in Bermuda

Here's what you don't get:

- **Shareholder rights.** No voting, no direct claim on underlying assets
- **EU, UK, Australia, Canada, or US access.** Geo-restricted
- **Traditional brokerage holding.** Can't withdraw to IBKR, only to compatible crypto wallets
- **Regulatory protections you'd have with a UCITS ETF.** Not covered by investor compensation schemes

Dividends are handled via a multiplier: your token balance increases proportionally when <code>SPY</code> distributes, rather than you receiving cash. Fine in theory.

Who are xStocks actually for? Investors in countries with limited access to global markets (parts of Latin America, Asia, Africa) for whom the alternative is no S&P 500 exposure at all. For anyone in the EU, UK, US, or Canada, they're a worse version of buying a regular ETF.

One legitimate use case: 24/7 price exposure. If you want to react to earnings or macro news outside US market hours, xStocks let you. But this is a *trading* use case, not a long-term investing one.

**Verdict: Skip unless you're in a jurisdiction without better options.**

:::

:::optional{CFDs}

### CFDs

Contracts for Difference let you bet on S&P 500 price movements — often with 20x leverage. Every CFD broker in Europe will cheerfully sell you one, with banners about the S&P 500 hitting new highs.

The EU mandates a standard disclaimer on CFD marketing: "74–89% of retail investor accounts lose money when trading CFDs with this provider." That number is usually accurate.

CFDs are not investments. They are short-term speculation instruments with asymmetric risk, overnight financing costs, and no claim on any underlying asset. Every long-term backtest shows that buy-and-hold S&P 500 beats leveraged CFD strategies by an embarrassing margin, even if you avoid liquidation.

**Verdict: Don't.**

:::

:::optional{Expensive mutual funds}

### European mutual funds labeled "S&P 500"

A quick warning for European investors. Many banks and insurers sell mutual funds with names like "US Large Cap Growth" or "American Equity" that charge 1.5–2.5% annual fees for what is essentially closet S&P 500 tracking. Over 30 years, a 1.5% higher fee on €100k compounds to more than €400k in lost wealth.

If a bank employee or financial advisor ever tries to sell you one of these, politely decline and walk out. The UCITS ETF market has solved this problem at 0.03–0.07%. Paying anything more for S&P 500 exposure is a tax on financial illiteracy.

**Verdict: Never.**

:::

:::optional{Leveraged ETFs}

### Leveraged ETFs (<code>UPRO</code>, <code>SSO</code>, <code>3LUS</code>, <code>CL2</code>)

<code>UPRO</code> targets 3x the daily return of the S&P 500. <code>SSO</code> targets 2x. Their European equivalents (<code>3LUS</code> from Lyxor, <code>CL2</code> from Amundi) do the same.

Over a single day, these work as advertised. Over longer periods, they don't.

The reason is volatility decay. Because leveraged ETFs rebalance daily, their long-term returns are path-dependent, not just direction-dependent. A simple example:

- Day 1: S&P 500 drops 10%. 2x ETF drops 20% (value: 80%)
- Day 2: S&P 500 rises 11.11% (back to 100%). 2x ETF rises 22.22% (value: 97.78%)

The index is flat, but the 2x ETF is down 2.2%. This happens every choppy day and it accumulates. In calm bull markets, leveraged ETFs can outperform. In volatile markets, they get destroyed. A 2008-style event takes <code>UPRO</code> down 80%+ and it rarely recovers to breakeven for years.

The math: backtests across 1955–2019 show that holding 2x or 3x leveraged S&P 500 *continuously* underperformed the un-leveraged index over most 20+ year windows. The recent bull market is an exception, not the rule.

**Verdict: Not for buy-and-hold.** If you want leverage, get a mortgage instead — it's cheaper, the decay effect doesn't exist, and the collateral is a house you can live in.

:::

:::optional{Options}

### Options

Options on the S&P 500 come in two flavors:

- **<code>SPX</code> options** — cash-settled European-style options on the index itself. Tax-advantaged in the US (Section 1256, 60/40 long/short-term treatment). Used by professionals for everything from hedging to income generation.
- **<code>SPY</code> options** — American-style options on the <code>SPY</code> ETF. Smaller contract size, less tax-efficient, but more flexible for retail.

For the purposes of this article, there are two legitimate uses of options for S&P 500 exposure:

**LEAPS for cheap leverage.** Buying a deep-in-the-money call option with 1–2 years to expiry gives you ~100 delta exposure to the S&P 500 for ~60–70% of the cost of the underlying shares. Effectively a 1.4–1.7x position without the daily rebalancing decay of leveraged ETFs. But you need to roll annually and you eat the bid-ask spread each time.

**Covered calls for income.** Selling out-of-the-money call options on your S&P 500 holdings generates premium income at the cost of capped upside. Works well in sideways markets, gets you wiped out (relatively speaking) in a strong rally.

These are not retirement strategies. They're tactical overlays that require active management, understanding of volatility surfaces, and honest evaluation of whether you have any edge at all. Most retail options traders don't, and lose to the market over time.

**Verdict: Leave options alone unless you're willing to treat them as a full-time hobby with real money risk.** The default S&P 500 ETF will outperform whatever you're about to try.

:::

## The one decision

Here's the entire article in one decision tree:

**If you're in the US:**
→ Fidelity customer? <code>FXAIX</code> or <code>FNILX</code>.
→ 401(k)? Lowest-cost S&P 500 fund in your plan.
→ Active trader? <code>SPY</code>.
→ Everyone else? **<code>VOO</code>.**

**If you're in Europe:**
→ Open your tax-advantaged account first (ISA, PEA, IKE, investeerimiskonto — whatever your country offers).
→ Default UCITS pick? **<code>SPYL</code>.**
→ Maximum performance, comfortable with swaps? **<code>SPXS</code>.**
→ Maximum liquidity? **<code>CSPX</code>.**
→ Want direct US ETF access for fees or sovereignty? See the [Sovereign Optimist essay](https://sovereignoptimist.substack.com/p/how-to-buy-us-etfs-as-a-european) — but check your country's US estate tax treaty first.

**If you're in a jurisdiction with limited broker access:**
→ Interactive Brokers if they serve you (best).
→ xStocks if they don't (acceptable fallback).

**If you're in Canada, Australia, or anywhere not covered above:**
→ Find the Irish-domiciled UCITS S&P 500 ETF with the lowest total cost of ownership for your broker. Probably <code>CSPX</code>, <code>VUAA</code>, or <code>SPYL</code>.
